The algorithm in plain words

At every station the truck looks one full tank ahead:

  1. if a cheaper station is within reach, buy just enough fuel to get there;
  2. otherwise, if the destination is within reach, buy just enough to finish;
  3. otherwise fill the tank and drive to the cheapest station within reach.

That is optimal: every mile is driven on fuel from the cheapest station that could have supplied it. The pure optimum sometimes stops for a couple of gallons to save a cent, so stops under — are merged with a neighbour when each fix costs at most —. Every stop of the final plan says where its fuel was planned by the greedy and what moving it cost, so the “Why” column of the Plan tab explains the plan you see, not the greedy's.

Design decisions and trade-offs

Offline geocoding instead of a geocoding API

Instead of: calling Nominatim or Google for every “City, ST”. Why: no call, no key, no rate limit, and the same place always gets the same point. Cost: city-level points; addresses fall back to Nominatim, whose match is named in a warning. Evidence: — places; this answer: —.

One routing call, as a compact polyline

Instead of: GeoJSON geometry, or one call per leg. Why: the brief asks for one call; a polyline is several times smaller. Evidence: — characters for — points.

Two caches and single flight

Why: a repeated trip costs no call, the other tank mode reuses the route, and identical requests in flight share one call. Cost: per process; several workers would share Redis. Evidence: this session made — OSRM calls for — new trips.

numpy in three passes instead of PostGIS or a KD-tree

Why: no extra service or dependency, and fast enough at this size. Cost: linear in the number of stations; a spatial index is the next step at scale. Evidence: — stations searched in —.

An exact greedy, checked against dynamic programming

Why: provably optimal for this problem, and cheap: O(n log n + n·k), with n the stations on the route and k those within one tank. Evidence: — stations in — on this trip; .

Consolidation with a cost cap

Why: drivers dislike a stop for a few gallons; the cap keeps the price of fewer stops visible and small. Evidence: — fewer stops for — on this trip.

Pay every mile by default; the range used literally

Why: “total money spent on fuel” for the trip should not depend on a free tank at the start. The truck leaves with a reserve and must arrive with the same amount: borrowed and returned, so the fuel bought equals the fuel burned. It is not a safety margin: between stops the full range is used, so a stop can be reached with an empty tank. start_tank=full is the alternative: a free full tank, and only what is needed to arrive is bought. Evidence: — bought for — burned; lowest arrival at a stop —.

Median price for stations listed several times

Why: the file quotes some stations several times; the median resists outliers and the spread stays visible. Evidence: — stations, policy —.

Decimal for money

Why: each cost is the rounded product of the shown gallons and price, and the total is their sum. Evidence: —/— contract checks in the Requirements tab.

Protect the free upstream

Why: the public OSRM and Nominatim servers have fair-use limits. How: connect and read timeouts, — retry for transient failures, an own rate limit of — requests per minute on /api/route (this page's own loads are not counted), and their rate limit turned into a clean answer with Retry-After.

How it was built

  1. Read the brief for what it asks directly (route, map, cheapest stops, total cost, a free routing API, few calls, fast answers) and between the lines (exact money, clear errors, messy data handled, tests).
  2. Data first: the price file has no coordinates, so an offline index of US places (Census Gazetteer and GeoNames) and a loader that merges repeated stations and places each one without any API.
  3. The core: one routing call decoded locally, the corridor search in numpy, the greedy optimizer, the endpoint and a first map, tested against brute force with the routing service faked.
  4. Then the real data pushed back: stations in the destination city, homonym cities (resolved from the address or left out), routes through Canada (a raster of the US outline), the empty-tank edge cases, and money in Decimal.
  5. Explaining the plan: the reason of each stop, the price-blind baseline and the strategy comparison, the record of how the plan was computed, Server-Timing, /api/stats and /api/about.
  6. This page, a client of the same API, with tests that keep it honest: no number written by hand, and every test, code link and requirement it cites exists on the server.
  7. A review from the page itself: what a reviewer could notice (a Canadian city planned as a street in Indiana, a stop explained by a station consolidation had removed, links that did not match GitHub) became fixes with regression tests.
