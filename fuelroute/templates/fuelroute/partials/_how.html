The algorithm in plain words

At every station the truck looks one full tank ahead:

  1. if a cheaper station is within reach, buy just enough fuel to get there;
  2. otherwise, if the destination is within reach, buy just enough to finish;
  3. otherwise fill the tank and drive to the cheapest station within reach.

That is optimal: every mile is driven on fuel from the cheapest station that could have supplied it. The pure optimum sometimes stops for a couple of gallons to save a cent, so stops under — are merged with a neighbour when each fix costs at most —. The Plan tab shows the price of that comfort.

Design decisions and trade-offs

Offline geocoding instead of a geocoding API

Instead of: calling Nominatim or Google for every “City, ST”. Why: no call, no key, no rate limit, and the same place always gets the same point. Cost: city-level points; addresses fall back to Nominatim. Evidence: — places; this answer: —.

One routing call, as a compact polyline

Instead of: GeoJSON geometry, or one call per leg. Why: the brief asks for one call; a polyline is several times smaller. Evidence: — characters for — points.

Two caches and single flight

Why: a repeated trip costs no call, the other tank mode reuses the route, and identical requests in flight share one call. Cost: per process; several workers would share Redis. Evidence: this session made — OSRM calls for — new trips.

numpy in three passes instead of PostGIS or a KD-tree

Why: no extra service or dependency, and fast enough at this size. Cost: linear in the number of stations; a spatial index is the next step at scale. Evidence: — stations searched in —.

An exact greedy, checked against dynamic programming

Why: linear time and provably optimal for this problem. Evidence: .

Consolidation with a cost cap

Why: drivers dislike a stop for a few gallons; the cap keeps the price of fewer stops visible and small. Evidence: — fewer stops for — on this trip.

Pay every mile by default

Why: “total money spent on fuel” for the trip should not depend on a free tank at the start. The truck leaves with a reserve and must return it; start_tank=full is the alternative. Evidence: — bought for — burned.

Median price for stations listed several times

Why: the file quotes some stations several times; the median resists outliers and the spread stays visible. Evidence: — stations, policy —.

Decimal for money

Why: each cost is the rounded product of the shown gallons and price, and the total is their sum. Evidence: —/— contract checks in the Requirements tab.

Protect the free upstream

Why: the public OSRM and Nominatim servers have fair-use limits. How: connect and read timeouts, — retry for transient failures, an own rate limit of — requests per minute, and their rate limit turned into a clean answer with Retry-After.

Known limits and next steps

  • Station coordinates are city-level: the farthest stop of this plan is — from the route as computed, which is approximate.
  • The detour to each stop is not priced: about — there and back on this plan (approx).
  • Some states have few or no stations in the file: — of the lower forty-eight have none.
  • The public OSRM server has no SLA: its p95 since this server started is —.
  • Caches and counters live in one process (pid —, up —).
  • The duration comes from OSRM's car profile, not a truck's.

Next steps: Redis for shared caches and exported metrics; an own OSRM with a truck profile; exact exit coordinates; a dated price feed; the time cost of a stop inside the objective; stops aligned with hours of service; a spatial index at scale.

How it was built

  1. Read the brief for what it asks directly (route, map, cheapest stops, total cost, a free routing API, few calls, fast answers) and between the lines (exact money, clear errors, messy data handled, tests).
  2. Studied the price file first: no coordinates, stations repeated with different prices, city names shared by several places. Built the offline place index and the homonym rules before writing the API.
  3. One routing call, decoded and resampled locally, cached by coordinates with single flight.
  4. The corridor search in numpy, checked against brute force.
  5. The optimizer: the classic greedy, checked against an exact dynamic program on random routes, then the capped consolidation.
  6. The response: Decimal money, every error with a code and the external calls already spent, Server-Timing, stats and about.
  7. This page: a client of the same API. Every number on it comes from an answer, from the server's counters or from the browser.
